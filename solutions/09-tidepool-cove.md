# Tidepool Cove

The ninth world, solved the same way and drawn the same way. A pearl takes the meadow's apple and a
jellyfish its skull, so an `a` below is a pearl worth five tiles to shut in, and an `x` a jellyfish
worth five fewer that no piece will build on — nothing drives a post through a sting. A `C` is the
crab, and the last field will not have the pig in with him, and will not call anything a win until
her ground has closed the whole way round his pool.

What the cove does differently is that every `~` below is **a broken ring**. A box of tidewater one
tile thick round a heart of dry sand, with exactly one tile of the lip missing where the tide got
out — never a corner, since nothing on a board walks diagonally. The heart is a finished pen and the
break is its door, which makes it the cheapest pen the game has ever offered. It is also the trap
the whole world is built on: a heart is small, the ring is where the tide put it, and half of what
the tide holds out it keeps — pearls sealed behind breaks that open onto the rim, and pearls lying
on the rim itself, where no pen can ever stand.

One field has no pool on it at all: The High Strand, the ground above the tideline, where every
tile is bought.

One pool with its break facing the rim and a pearl sealed in its heart, two jellyfish stranded in
its lee, and two more pearls lying on the very line of the map. Nothing on this board can be won
but ground, and the board is how the cove says so.

The block a player squares off in the pool's lee spends thirteen pieces holding twenty tiles and
both stings, which is 10. The same thirteen drawn as a diamond on the open sand hold 27 tiles and
the same pair — a diamond wide enough to be worth drawing cannot come down between them, and ten
points is cheaper than the ground the swerve would give up — which is 17, the smallest opening
maximum in the game and the world's first lesson priced exactly.

Two small pools set corner to corner with their breaks turned in towards each other, and the pig on
the open sand between them. The span question: neither ring walls anything on its own, and no
rectangle reaches round both.

A pearl lies where each pool argues for the band. One is out on the open sand off the north-east
shoulder of the first ring, far enough that only a long wall thrown between the pools ever comes
near it; the other is in the heart of the second ring, which is bought by stepping in through the
pool's own break. Squaring off can bend far enough to pick up one of them — 17 tiles and the pearl,
which is 22 — and no rectangle on this board gets both.

The twelve pieces run instead as one long diagonal band from the north-east corner of the board
down between the two rings, leaning on the outside of one going down and the other coming back,
and it collects both pearls on the way: 29 tiles and the pair, which is 39. The hearts of these
pools were the cheapest ground on the board and the least of it; putting a pearl in one is what
makes the cheap ground worth the walk.

The ground above the tideline: dry strand twelve tiles deep, no pool anywhere on it, and the
biggest budget in the world laid out across it. It is The Hardpan again, and The Blind Grike and
Swept Flat before it — nothing free, and the diamond is the answer it always was.

What bends it is a pearl in each far corner, one out to the north-east and one down to the
south-west, and a wrack line of three jellyfish laid on the diagonal between the pig and the
nearer of them — the last tide's leavings dropped in a row, the way a tide actually drops them.
A rectangle reaching for that pearl has to cross the wrack and swallow it, and a rectangle nudged
off one jellyfish walks onto the next and then onto the rim. So squaring off is held to a four by
five holding 20, and it reaches neither pearl.

The same nineteen pieces run round as a diamond pulled out of true to the north-east and hold 31
tiles and the pearl up there: 36. The south-western one stays where the tide left it, behind the
wrack — on ground with no free wall on it a pearl is four pieces of fence, and four pieces of
diamond are worth more than a pearl. The strand asks which one, not both.

One pool with its break turned east and two pearls seeded in its heart, a third loose on the sand,
and the pig in the far corner. The detour question asked through the break: the heart's only door
faces away from everything, so the pen that wants the pearls comes the whole way round the pool
and in at the east side.

The block leans the west rim and takes the loose pearl for 23. The lozenge holds 27 tiles and all
three pearls, which is 42 — ground given up for ground, which is what a detour is, and the first
heart in the world worth buying.

One pool the width of the strand with its break mid-lip, two jellyfish stranded under its south
wall, and two pearls further down the sand. The shore question on the longest bank the cove has:
the lip is eight tiles of free wall, and the whole field is how much of it to use and what using

One great pool along the whole north of the board with two pearls teasing behind a rim-facing
break, a second pool down the east side with its break turned in, and ten pieces — the corner
question with two free sides, and at 53% the hardest corner anywhere in the game.

The right-angled answer tucks a rectangle under the north pool and pays its own east and south
walls: 22. The chevron runs from under the north lip down to the east pool's flank with every
piece on the diagonal, holds 42 tiles, and lets itself in at the east pool's break for the pearl
on the way: 47. The north pool's pearls stay where they are — nine tiles of heart and treasure
with no door anybody can use, which is the loudest tease in the world.

## 9. The Crab Pool — 22 pieces, 52

```
#aoo~C~ooa#

```

The cove's boss, and the ninth rule the game has: the crab keeps his pool, and the pig's ground
has to close the whole way round it — break, crab and all. One piece on the break shuts him into
his heart, the cheapest boss pen the game has ever handed out, and every other piece goes on a
moat that no way off the map may miss.

The answers eight worlds have taught are turned down one after the other. Run the ring and forget
the plug and the crab strolls out of his pool into the pig's own ground — held, and held together,
which is no answer. Plug the break and box the pig beside the pool and both are held in two tidy
pens, which is no ring. The square moat with the plug is the obvious pen and worth 17 of 52, and
it reaches not one of the three pearls.

The same twenty-two pieces swung out as a diamond hold 37 tiles and all three — one lying off each
face of the square's reach, which is exactly where a diamond's points go. At 67% it was the widest
gap any board in the game left until the tundra's Haulout asked 69. It asks for 21 of the 24 stars
below it before it opens.
