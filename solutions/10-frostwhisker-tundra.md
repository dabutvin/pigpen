# Frostwhisker Tundra

The tenth world, solved the same way and drawn the same way. A ski takes the meadow's apple and a
ice slick its skull, so an `a` below is a ski worth five tiles to shut in, and an `x` an ice slick worth five
fewer that no piece will build on — nothing drives a post through blue ice. An `L` is the bull
seal, and the last field will not have the pig in with him, and will not call anything a win until
his own pen lies against the water.

What the tundra does differently is that every `~` below is **a pressure ridge**: ice crushed
upward where two floes met, in a straight chain of single tiles on the diagonal, at least three
long, corner touching corner and never two abreast. Each blade seals the ground it corner-touches,
so a ridge is a diagonal wall laid for free — and a diagonal wall is the best wall there is, which
every answer below has been saying since the meadow's Dew Ponds. There is no heart to fall for and
no mouth to plug up here; the whole question, nine times over, is which stretch of the free
diagonal to finish and what shape finishes it.

One field has no ice on it at all: The Whiteout, where the weather has come down and every tile is
bought.

Two ridges leaning together like a roof, with a one-tile gap at the apex and two ice slicks sheeted
across the bowl between them. Both answers buy the apex plug — it is the cheapest wall on the board
— and both take the ice slicks with it, because going round them costs more ground than their ten
points.

The block squares the bowl off at the ridges' feet for 32 tiles less the ice slicks, which is 22. The
same thirteen pieces flare on south past the feet, lean the outside of both ridges the whole way
down, and hold 43 tiles, the ski on the open snow and the same two ice slicks, which is 38. Two more
skis lie frozen in along the south rim, where nothing can ever be penned.

The weather down on the ground: no ridge anywhere, and the biggest budget in the world laid
across bare snow. It is The High Strand and The Hardpan again — and what it adds to them is that
the snow is not empty.

Three ice slicks stand in a line up column five and out to the bottom rim, and a fourth shuts off the
east. A block that tries to square off across that line has to swallow an ice slick, and a block nudged
off an ice slick walks onto the next one and then onto the rim, where there is nothing left to bump out
to — so every tidy answer is driven west, and the best of them holds 20 tiles and no ski at all.

Two of the three skis are frozen in along the rims, in plain sight and out of reach for good: that
is the whiteout's own joke, the catch you can see through the weather and never get to. The third
is down on the ice within reach, and the nineteen pieces run round as a diamond pulled out of true
to the south-west to take it — 31 tiles and the ski, which is 36. On ground with nothing free on
it, the only question left is which way to lean, and the one ski that can be had is the answer.

Three ridges scattered to three quarters of the compass — the whiskers the world is named for —
and every one of them an arc of the same diamond ring drawn at five paces round the pig. The
budget is exactly the ten mud tiles of that ring the ice did not build: see the whole shape and
the whole shape is affordable, see anything less and it is not. The only affordable block is the
bottom half-diamond between the south ridges, 21; the ring holds 41 tiles and the two skis in its
top half, which is 51. At 58% it is the widest gap any field outside a boss leaves in the game,
past Starwell Ring's 56 — the Dew Ponds' lesson, spaced out to the width of a world that has been
teaching diagonals for nine fields.

The tundra's boss, and the tenth rule the game has: the bull seal will not share a pen with the
pig, and the pen he is given has to lie against the water — he keeps a breathing hole. The board
hands him the one corner with no ice anywhere near it, which is the whole trap: box him tidily
where he lies and the board sends it back, held and held dry, which for a seal is no holding at
all.

The answers nine worlds have drilled are turned down one after the other. One great pen round the
pair holds 51 tiles — more ground than the winning answer — and is refused whole, because the
seal will not share. Two tidy boxes are refused too, because the seal's never touches the water.
The tidy answer that satisfies the rule boxes the pig on her own tile and walls the seal a yard
of ice against the great ridge's tail, where the sea shows through: 13 of 42.

The same seventeen pieces run down the great ridge's whole west flank instead — the longest free
wall in the world doing the holding — with a two-piece yard for the seal against the same tail,
and hold 42 tiles, the ski and the ice slick, which is 42. At 69% it was the widest gap any board in
the game left until the fen's Wallow asked 72. It asks for 21 of the 24 stars below it before it
opens.
