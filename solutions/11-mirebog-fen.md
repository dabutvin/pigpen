# Mirebog Fen

The eleventh world, solved the same way and drawn the same way. A lotus flower takes the meadow's
apple and a mosquito its skull, so an `a` below is a lotus flower worth five tiles to shut in, and an
`x` a mosquito worth five fewer that no piece will build on — no wall on the fen keeps a mosquito off.
A `G` is the old croc, and the last field will not have the pig in with him, and will not call
anything a win until every bank of one whole channel is his own ground.

What the fen does differently is that every `~` below is **a closed braid**: a channel that
splits, rejoins, and rings its own ground, never running two tiles deep. Its jogged outer bank
is a free wall no rectangle can follow, and the dry bars inside its loops are ground nothing
can ever walk to — so a lotus flower parked on a bar is the loudest tease since the cove's rim
seashells, and there are four of them across the trail: in plain sight, five points, and nobody's.
The fen keeps what it rings. What it does not keep it pays out, though: every board's best pen
below wins at least one lotus flower, which no world before this one could promise.

One field has no channel on it at all: The Turbary, the cut-over peat, where every tile is
bought.

Two staircase braids leaning together into an arch over the pig's hollow, and the water has
nearly done it: both flanks of the bowl are walled for free and one piece at the apex shuts
the mouth. The block plugs the apex too and squares the bowl off with a straight row — but the
mosquito sits exactly where its second row wants to be, so it stops one row short at 21.

The same eleven pieces run the south wall out as a deep V instead, scoop the west lotus flower on
the way down, and hold 32 tiles and the flower, which is 37. The east lotus flower is the gift not
worth taking: reaching both costs more ground than one is worth, which is the fen's opening
lesson priced exactly.

The cut-over ground: no channel anywhere, the pig pushed west, and the biggest budget in the

five linked eyes, its long south bank the dominant free wall — the whole field is which

The corner of the fen, asked with the same scatter of flowers as Bilberry Hags one field
earlier — on purpose, since the hags ask whether the flowers are worth bowing for and the

mosquito.

The broadest board in the fen: the north-west carr and its south-east mirror thrown as far

The fen's boss, and the eleventh rule the game has: the old croc will not share a pen with the
pig, and the pen he is given has to hold every bank of one whole channel — a croc keeps a
wallow rather than visits one, and half a wallow is nobody's. His channel is the only one on
the board whose banks can all be walked — its three sisters are backed against the rims — and
he lies in the one-tile causeway beside it, water on both hands.

The answers ten worlds have taught are turned down one after the other. Box him in his
causeway with two pieces — water walls the other two sides — and both animals are held, and
the board sends it back: his pen laps one bank of the wallow and not the rest. Pen the pair
together in one sweep of the east fen — 49 tiles, more ground than the winning answer — and
that comes back whole, because the croc is not company. The tidy answer that satisfies the
rule boxes the pig on her own tile and rings his channel tight, leaning the sister braids for
walls, and the mosquito staked on the ring's south line makes even that answer pay: 13 of 48.

The same nineteen pieces swing the ring wide instead and take the whole east fen into the
croc's ground — both live lotus flowers, both mosquitos, and the causeway he lies in. At 72% it was
the widest gap any board in the game left until the heights' Eyrie asked 76. The lotus flower on his channel's own bar stays
where it is, which on this board is the croc's pantry. It asks for 21 of the 24 stars below
it before it opens.
